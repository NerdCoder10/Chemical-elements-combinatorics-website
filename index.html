How the results are produced

Every combination falls into one of these cases. The badge on each result tells you which one you are looking at.

Tabulated
The compound is in the database with measured ΔHf° and S°. The site balances the formation reaction (elements in their standard states → compound) and computes ΔH° = Σ ν ΔHf°(products) − Σ ν ΔHf°(reactants) and ΔS° = Σ ν S°(products) − Σ ν S°(reactants). Elements in their standard states have ΔHf° = 0 by definition.
ΔS estimated
ΔHf° is measured, but S° of the compound is not in the tables used, so S° is estimated by adding up per-atom contributions.
Estimated
No tabulated compound. A formula is predicted from the most common oxidation states. ΔH is estimated with Pauling's electronegativity relation (ΔHf ≈ −96.5 kJ/mol × bonds × Δχ²), and ΔS with additive atomic entropies. These are order-of-magnitude values only, and the predicted compound may not exist in exactly that form.
Computed (DFT)
A theoretical result from density functional theory (JARVIS-DFT, NIST, OptB88vdW functional), available for 32,619 element combinations and 65,307 compounds. ΔH is the computed formation energy (at about 0 K). Whether a compound is stable is decided by the convex hull: the compound must be lower in energy than every mixture of competing compounds with the same overall composition. This site recomputes that hull itself from the formation energies. ΔS is an additive estimate. The Theory explorer tab shows how well these predictions match experiment.
No reaction
Noble gases (apart from a few xenon and krypton compounds), or an element combined with itself when it has no other allotrope. Nothing changes, so ΔH° = ΔS° = 0.
Nuclear
Only when you pick specific isotopes. Fusion and transmutation reactions are shown with their Q-value. ΔH is −Q per mole of events. These energies are about a million times larger than chemical ones.

Things to keep in mind

  • Thermodynamics is not kinetics. A negative ΔG° says a reaction is favourable, not that it is fast. Diamond is less stable than graphite and still lasts forever.
  • Isotopes barely change chemistry. Different isotopes of an element share the same electron structure. Effects on ΔH are typically well below 1 %. Hydrogen is the exception: D₂O is shown separately.
  • ΔG at other temperatures uses ΔG(T) ≈ ΔH° − TΔS°, treating ΔH° and ΔS° as constant. This is reasonable for a few hundred kelvin and ignores phase changes.
  • Elements 101–118 have only ever been made a few atoms at a time, so they have no measurable bulk thermochemistry.
  • Isotopes: all 3,339 nuclides that have ever been observed are listed, with half-life, decay modes, natural abundance, atomic mass and discovery year from NUBASE2020. Most exist for microseconds or less. The element panel shows the notable ones first; use “Show all” for the full list. Half-lives marked “~” are estimates from systematics.
